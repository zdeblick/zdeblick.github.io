I want to return to our Big Question: what should we optimize for when designing our system of government? I'll make the point that at least part of what we *should* optimize for is the thing that history naturally selects for, stability. Then I'll ask how stable different forms of government are, focusing on differences between autocracies and democracies. 

First, what the heck do I mean by saying "that history naturally selects for" as if governments are things that evolve through natural selection and are not designed by wise founding fathers intelligently designing a principled system? While the two ideas of a random process and an intelligent designer as originator are usually at odds when it comes to the origin of humanity, in general they needn't be (and I would say they shouldn't be when it comes to the origin of humanity either, but that's not today's argument). The process of evolution can be understood as an optimization algorithm that is especially good at searching a very high-dimensional and discrete space of possibilities. In fact, computer scientists use versions of it all the time to optimize for various things of interest to computer scientists (my final project of an AI class that I did with my friend Jacob was designing an artificial Pac-Man-like species to be good at ["Pac-War"](https://www.clear.rice.edu/comp440/old/fall2007/pac.html)). The process is generally defined by a three-stage process that a population of individuals undergoes:

* Selection: individuals are evaluated for fitness
* Reproduction: parent individuals are combined to generate offspring individuals, with the fittest parents over-represented in production of offspring
* Mutation: offspring are randomly altered

Crucially, terms like "fitness", "over-represented", "individual", and "randomly altered" can mean any number of things, depending on the optimization problem. When we talk about *Natural* Selection, we generally mean that "fitness" means "capability to survive and reproduce in the natural environment" (in the artificial case it can mean basically whatever the computer scientist wants to optimize for). Two important points for our discussion:

* Just because there are random parts of the process does not mean there cannot be a role for intelligence, for example in making choices about how exactly to combine different parents during reproduction (breeding is evolution too).
* "Individual" can really mean anything: an individual organism, an individual protein, an individual Pac-Creature, an individual idea, or an individual society.

In his 1976 book, "The Selfish Gene," Richard Dawkins proposes the term "meme" for an individual idea that undergoes an evolutionary process, multiplying and alterning itself to compete for space in human minds. Crucially, the "fitness" of a meme does not depend only on whether it is a good idea, but on how catchy it is (like how you catch a cold). Just as most music listeners (not myself but I think most) differentiate between good music and catchy music, brains tend to latch on to catchy ideas and not just good ones. I think it's amusing how Dawkins' own meme, the "meme", has taken on a life of its own, with its vernacular meaning having much more to do with the catchiness of the idea than its quality. And although the creation and transmission of memes is influenced by non-random, intelligent humans, it can still be accurately described as an evolutionary process.

Today I want to focus on one specific type of memes: those that are codified in constitutions, legal codes, and the practical functioning of governments around the world. Undoubtedly these are memes: every new constitution that is written draws on pre-existing constitutions, laws, and norms, and, as we discussed in [the last post](https://zdeblick.github.io/2025/02/24/the-first-sich.html), democratic traditions have been evolving at the level of towns for thousands of years. However, the survival of these memes is tightly linked to the survival of the governments which employ them. In turn, many such memes are never removed from a given state's legal apparatus, and so they can be taken as part of that state's *identity*. The upshot of all this is that we would expect evolution to select for those forms of government that lead to stable regimes. However, their meme-quality suggests that their survival also depends on how catchy they are - an idea that I would say is closely related to the idea from last post that democratic culture is vital for the survival of democracies. It is also related to the idea that simpler methods of government might be easier to adopt.

If we think of forms of government as a thing undergoing an evolutionary process, we must accept that it has not had much time to evolve in an environment similar to today's. New national constitutions are not written all that often, and the first one was written relatively few generations ago (generations of states, not humans). While evolution of these memes did occur at the level of towns and in the monarchies that dominated history, human society and technology has evolved much faster (just take a look at congress questioning social media CEOs). Additionally, the populations of states served by constitutions is orders of magnitude larger than those in which legal memes largely evolved (more on this point in my next post). For now, the main implication of all this is that many legal memes may be maladaptive. Maybe they worked for a town of a hundred in 1020, but that doesn't mean they work for a nation of hundreds of *millions* in 2020.

I think this perspective helps make sense of one of the main findings in political science about the relationship between regime type and stability. [Håvard Hegre, T. Ellingsen, S. Gates, and Petter Gleditsch's original study in 2001](https://scispace.com/pdf/toward-a-democratic-civil-peace-democracy-political-change-21xpuv3uyy.pdf) found that, of states existing between 1816-1992, the most unstable type of regime is not fully democratic or fully autocratic, but somewhere in the middle (with democracies being more stable than autocracies). This makes sense if we think about autocracy and democracy as two incompatible meme complexes that compete with each other for control of individual states, as well as individual minds. Strong democracies have established rule of law, including an electoral system, and citizens who believe firmly in those institutions and their right and responsibility to participate. Strong autocracies have strong individual leaders with consolidated power and citizens who believe that their leader is good and right, and any citizens who think otherwise are quickly silenced. Autocracies continue to be autocracies until there are enough citizens who lose faith in the leader and are brave enough to say so, and democracies continue to be democracies until one leader manages to consolidate enough support that they can dissolve any institutions that check their power. Each of these things is more likely to happen when the democratic systems and values or the strong leader and their grip on power are not as firmly established. If this trend continues for long enough, we should expect to see more of a binary distinction between autocracies and democracies emerge, through a process reminiscent of speciation. 

I have one last thought on the observation that democracies tend to be more stable than autocracies. Obviously advantages that democracies have over autocracies that may contribute to stability, especially related to how the rule of law fosters a strong economy. But one other fact that likely contributes to this is that democracies tend not to go to war against each other, while autocracies will go to war with either type (see [this article](https://www.homeworkforyou.com/static_media/uploadedfiles/Classical%20Liberals.pdf) for example). This makes sense, given that democracies ascribe power to the common people and systems that coordinate that power. While different democracies have different people and different systems, any competition between the legal memes concerns how one system or the other *more effectively represents the will of the people*. In this way, democracies can help each other improve by sharing and adopting methods without completely dissolving the state. Autocracies, however, ascribe power to *their individual leader's abilities*, including their ability to strongarm the leaders of other states. The important difference is that this leader is different for different autocracies, and, while any two autocracies share memes about the importance of having a strong leader, only one leader can be stronger. There is no way to reform one leader into another, unless by reform you "mean kill and replace with a loyalist". So this is the natural course that autocracies run, and leaders are able to prevent such aggression from destabilizing their regime in part by controlling the information citizens recieve so that their state-sponsored memes face little competition in their minds. Provided the aggression is successful, this can also be used to further stabilize society by providing further evidence that the leader is strong and using the spoils of war to make the public wealthier.

The fact that democracies seem more stable than autocracies should not be taken to mean that the ultimate triumph of democracy is inevitable. Democracies stability depends in part on their superior ability to work together, an advantage that may be lessening. If democracies surrounded by other democracies get too complacent about their collective security and skimp on defense, and autocracies are able to maintain a long enough alliance, they may be temporarily strong enough to conquer many (otherwise more stable) democracies in short order. This is essentially how WWII happened, and the Allies winning was not inevitible, and certainly depended on the failed alliance between Nazi Germany and the USSR. We, in democratic countries, need to continue working together to exchange ideas about better governance and strengthen our collective defense, so that we can continue to evolve more stable societies without getting destroyed by autocrats who must try to do so to maintain their illusion of strength.
