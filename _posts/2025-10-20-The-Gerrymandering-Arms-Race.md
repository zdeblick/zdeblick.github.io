

I know I've already written a post mostly about gerrymandering, but a lot has happened in the US regarding gerrymandering since then, I've learned more stuff that I want to share, and California voters face a horrible decision in a couple weeks that I hope to help inform.

For those who didn't read my [previous post](https://zdeblick.github.io/2025/03/26/Representation.html), I would still recommend it. If you don't want to, or did read and want a quick reminder, here's a rough summary:

* Representing the will of the people in a national government's actions is a difficult problem that the USA solves fairly well.
* The US House of Representatives is designed to achieve accurate representation.
* Even though our current system, single member districts, is better than our initial system, it is not very accurate in general.
* Generally, single member districts tend to amplify majorities
* The accuracy of a given map depends on the politics: in one election it might produce a fair outcome, while another election with the same maps could lead to a very unfair outcome (even assuming voters do not move).
* With the current balance of power in America, the nationwide Democrat-Republican inaccuracy is not too bad since gerrymandering in majority-Dem states mostly cancels out that in majority-Rep states.
* We should improve our system by introducing better systems like Single Transferrable Vote, making coordinated efforts between Rep and Dem states (or nationally) so that these improvements do not favor one party or the other.

Unfortunately, that last bullet point isn't exactly happening. Instead, we have the opposite: a gerrymandering arms race. It is not my goal here to assign blame to individual states or politicians, but rather to understand what is happening and think about the best path forward, especially for California voters like myself, faced with a decision on Prop 50. 

**The Arms Race**
To make the arms race metaphor more concrete, we need to first understand a key concept in game theory, the prisoner's dilemma:

You and a co-conspirator have been arrested for a crime, but the prosecution only has enough evidence to get either of you on minor charges carrying a 1-year sentence. However, they're willing to make a deal with you: testify against your partner and they'll drop the minor charges against you. You are unable to communicate with your partner, but you assume she is being offered the same deal. What should you do? 

Suppose your parnter does testify against you. Then you will be convicted of major charges from their testimony that carry a 5-year sentence. If you don't testify against your partner, it will be a 6-year sentence (including the minor charges); if you do, it wil only be 5 years. Clearly testifying is better for you.

Suppose your partner does not testify against you. If you don't testify against her, you will serve 1 year; if you do, you will walk free. Clearly testifying is better for you.

So, if both you and your partner behave rationally, you will both testify and serve 5 years. This is frustrating for both of you, sure, since if you *cooperated* with each other, you would both only get 1 year. But with no outside mechanism allowing you to trust each other, what else is there to do?

Fortunately, in the real world there are often outside mechanisms. For one thing, decisions like this one are often made not just once, but repeatedly over time. Sometimes, deciders refuse to cooperate over and over, as in a conventional arms race. The idea with an arms race is that if one state is more powerful, militarily, than a neighbor, it can coerce that neighbor and gain some advantage from that coersion. However, funding a powerful military is costly. If the perceived advantage from coersion outweighs this cost, both states may choose to arm, even though both arming will not change the balance of power. Thus both states pay the cost with no reward, even though they could avoid it by coordinating and not arming.

Usually, however, states and other actors are able to coordinate by following conventions where they will cooperate, so long as everyone else cooperates. Then it is in the interest of all parties to cooperate, since any short term gains made by disobeying while others still cooperate would be outweighed by the loss of future gains when others retaliate. Another factor supporting cooperation is reputation: if you try to cheat somebody today, the person you try to do a deal with tomorrow may have heard about it and cheat you in return. In general, the presence of future deals and the potential for the present to inform actions in those future deals leads to cooperation.

So why do arms races happen, either conventional or political? One answer already hides in our explanation of cooperation: if the future gains of cooperation *do not* outweigh the short-term benefits of non-cooperation, than parties may not cooperate. This could look like one state militarizing very quickly to conquer a neighbor before that neighbor has time to respond. Or it could look like one party gerrymandering a lot because, even though it is worse if everybody gerrymanders, that party thinks doing so will give them a decisive edge in the next election that outweighs that cost.

Another answer is a mechanism known as a principal-agent problem. This reflects the idea that the people making these important decisions do not feel the costs that everyone else will. Specifically, our representatives (agents) are nominally supposed to make decisions that benefit their constituents (principals), but they often have conflicting interests. Eisenhower pointed out how these can lead to conventional arm races by identifying the "Defense Industrial Complex," wherein politicians receive funding from defense companies to win elections and in return those politicians authorize more military spending than would otherwise be appropriate. Even though taxpayers have to pay for that military spending and costs associated with drawing adversarial countries into an arms race, the politicians keep winning elections and the defense companies keep making money so the decision makers are happy. This can lead to gerrymandering races as well--even if we all accepted that gerrymandering was bad for our country overall, there is one group of people it is very good for: incumbent politicians in the majority party. For them, the power to draw district maps gives them more job security by setting up elections where they, personally, are very likely to remain in office.

**Today's mess**

This brings me to the current situation, where several Republican-controlled states have sought to gerrymander more seats, California has responded, and other Republican-controlled states are responding to that. California voters must now decide whether they want to be part of the arms race, or back out. I completely understand the rationale behind a NO vote, arguing in favor of initiating cooperation with other states and avoiding the adverse effects of entrenched incumbents and less responsive Representatives. However, this does not seem like a moment when Republican-controlled states are itching to cooperate and if Prop 50 does not pass, there is little reason to think that Texas and others will undo their gerrymanders. Also, I don't see any reason to believe that the incumbency advantage would be any stronger under the proposed map.

It's worth asking if California's proposal overcompensates by gerrymandering more seats than are being gerrymandered elsewhere, thus making the nationwide situation less fair. After my post on representation, I dug into some more data from other years to get a better sense of how much the results in that post depended on the choice I made to use the 2020 election data and maps. Since today we're focused on gerrymandering that's happening after the 2024 election, let's focus on the maps used in 2024 and any new state maps since 2024. In particular, there are two relevant questions concerning the balance of power:
* Did the 2024 maps favor Republicans or Democrats?
* Does the proposed California map shift the balance of power by more than the other gerrymanders favoring Republicans?
