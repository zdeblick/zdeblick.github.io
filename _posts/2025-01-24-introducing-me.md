Introducing Me (and this blog)

Hi, I'm Daniel, and welcome to my blog. I'm a scientist and engineer by training, and have recently embarked on a journey of using tools from this world to understand and improve democratic institutions. This blog serves as a platform for me to share my ideas and some examples of how we can use such tools to shed light on specific phenomena that occur in democracies (and maybe in other countries as well, and in interactions between them). The main purpose of this post is to talk about who I am and what brought me here. The motivation for this is not (I hope) narcissism, but a desire to share what is important to me about this work and an obligation to inform you about the personal bias I bring. My wife studied education, and scholars in that field would call this a "positionality statement," which is just academic jargon for "positioning" the author of a research paper as a person within the context of what they're talking about, so that the reader can make better judgments about personal bias. This is not common practice in political science, even though we understand that political ideology can have a huge effect on how we process new information through motivated reasoning (more on this in a later post). Anyways, this is all to say that I think it's important for me to do, especially as this blog is not subject to any peer review process.

So what is my political "position"? I like to categorize myself as a "radical centrist," by which I mean that I have moderate views on many issues and do not always agree with either party, but believe in institutional reform (that's the "radical" part). I have to say that, in today's America, I find myself agreeing with the Democrats more often than with the Republicans, and I even drove 4 hours from the San Francisco Bay Area to Reno 3 separate weekends to help the local Democratic campaign convince and turn out voters. However, I crossed out the party's recommendation to vote against the ranked-choice voting proposition on my pamphlets and told anyone who was interested (and probably many others) that I disagreed with it. I have also been incredibly frustrated with the recent Democratic leadership for their handling of russia's (lower case is an intentional sign of disrespect for the terrorist state) invasion of Ukraine and poor communication with the American people, as well as attempts at partisan backstabbing, such as House Democrats' decision to side with the most extreme Republicans in ousting Speaker McCarthy. In short, I don't think either major party represents me very well, probably because they spend so much effort using issues we disagree on to convince Americans to hate the other side instead of making compromises and passing legislation that's in the interest of all Americans.

It's also worth sharing that my path to this political orientation has a lot to do with my family. My dad is a Ukrainian-American conservative from Chicago, and my mom is a more liberal New Yorker. I don't want to get too deep into my family, other than to say that through political discussions in my family, as well as my dad's extended family, I learned that politics was not a safe subject of conversation: people disagree too strongly over the issues, the emotions attached are too strong for anything productive to come from discussion, and more likely than not people will just have their feelings hurt. I also had the belief that there was no real need to talk about politics: nobody seemed to really like the government, but I didn't see how any policy negatively impacted me, and there wasn't a lot to do about it anyways. So I grew up intentionally apathetic, I didn't vote, and I certainly didn't engage in political discussions. I built a wall around myself and focused on being good at school and things I enjoyed, like soccer.

This wall did not come crumbling down all at once — I would say that it was more of a gradual process of erosion. But there are three specific events that dealt heavy damage to it: the Black Lives Matter protests in 2020, Donald Trump's claims that he won the 2020 election and the January 6 mob, and russia's invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Each of these events showed me a specific way in which people in positions of power and the systems they exist within were letting people down, miserably. Before reading "The New Jim Crow" after the murder of George Floyd, I did not realize how badly and how designedly badly American institutions, especially the criminal justice system, were still treating Black people. Before January 6, I took our peaceful transition of power and respect of electoral results completely for granted. And before February 24, 2022, I thought I lived in a world where dictators could no longer get away with declaring their intent to destroy the national identity of a neighboring democracy and then attempting to do so through invasion and acts of genocide for no other reason than that they wanted to. Certainly, this last event struck me close to home, as I possess some of that identity which Putin seeks to destroy. I have no doubt that if he could destroy me without any consequences, he would.

Another theme that these events share in common is how differently the two sides of the political spectrum perceived them. My wife and I lived in Seattle during the BLM protests in 2020, maybe a 15 minute drive from the Capitol Hill occupied protest, and the contrast between what we experienced and the genuine concern we received from our more conservative relatives was stark, to say the least. With russia's invasion, it initially seemed that America was uniformly behind Ukraine's defense, even if there was some dithering from the Biden administration. But it didn't take too long before many on the right decided it would be politically useful to them to downplay the importance of Ukraine's defense and sabotage our support. At its worst, this includes politicians like Tulsi Gabbard and Marjorie Taylor Greene spouting russian propaganda to American constituents in an attempt to warp their perceived reality for their own benefit. January 6, however, provides the clearest picture of how different the worlds we live in are. To me, it is clear that Biden won the election, and Trump continues to lie about it for his own political gain, but to many of those who stormed the Capitol, it is clear that Trump won the election, and they themselves are heroic defenders of democracy, right up there with protestors in authoritarian regimes. That such violent disagreement can arise over such a factual matter is a damning verdict on how low our partisan antipathy has brought us.

As I write this, it is a couple of days after Trump's second inauguration and his pardoning or commuting of all January 6 sentences. This news made me sad, mostly, and angry. But in thinking about the January 6 protestors, I cannot feel angry at them, because even though I believe they are personally responsible for their own understanding of the world and their own decisions about whom to trust and how to act, I also believe that many of them genuinely thought they were doing the right thing, and the horrible thing they did is in large part the product of their environment. They trusted Trump because he was their president, and he lied to them, and as such he is responsible for January 6.

However, I accept that our judicial system did not declare him ineligible to hold the office again and that voters elected him president. To do otherwise would be just as bad as what Trump did in claiming he won in 2020. I may disagree with him on many matters, I may think he has poor moral character, I may have worked earnestly to prevent him from winning, but he is my president now. This does not mean I will accept anything he does — it means I will hold him accountable as my president, as I hope that all Americans will join me in doing, whatever their political ideology.

That being said, I don't think it is good enough to protest his entire platform. I was looking for a local protest against Maduro's inauguration as President of Venezuela on January 10, and struggled to find one even as I accidentally found scores of protests against Trump's inauguration. Maduro actually stole an election (for now it seems this is something we all agree on), the protestors in Venezuela are actually heroes who risk their lives for their democracy, and Trump's inauguration was a full 10 days later. So even though I don't agree with Trump's platform, and even though I don't think his 1.47% margin of victory in the popular vote represents a landslide with a sweeping mandate, I do think that voters voted for his platform and we should focus our effort on specific things we can effect, like the process for confirming Tulsi Gabbard as director of national intelligence and Trump's unconstitutional executive order to revoke birthright citizenship. Because even though few of us have the power to individually change what our government does, when we work together and pick concrete targets that have strong popular and/or legal support, sometimes we can.

The picture this all leads me to and compels me to act is that we are currently in a struggle for democracy, against autocracy. This battle is against many individual autocrats, like Putin and Maduro, but also against systemic problems like those in our criminal justice system that give rise to disproportionate punishment of Black people, and those in our electoral and media systems that give rise to extreme polarization of our two parties and their supporters. Ukraine is a frontline of this struggle, but so is the dinner table, where we work towards a civil discussion of ideas on which we disagree and a path towards compromise.

I'm currently engaged in two specific organizations that seek to improve systems that give rise to our polarized dysfunction. The first, [CommonSense American](https://www.commonsenseamerican.org/), works at the national level to provide input from CommonSense Americans (like me) from across the political spectrum on upcoming legislation that has bipartisan support but not necessarily enough attention or clarity for congress to stop bickering and actually pass it. The other, [CalRCV](https://www.calrcv.org/), is working to implement ranked-choice voting in local California elections, and I'm helping them bring it to Redwood City. CalRCV has [sister-organizations](https://fairvoteaction.org/get-involved/state_based_rcv_groups/) in most states under the umbrella of FairVote, and there are plenty of other local organizations working to strengthen local government that you can help, like the League of Women Voters. And CommonSense American is part of [Braver Angels](https://braverangels.org/), a broader constellation of national organizations working to combat hyperpartisanship in congress and at the dinner table. 

And I hope that this blog will be another way I can contribute to this struggle by sharing what I learn with y'all. To start, here is an up-to-date list of other sources I follow for analysis of our political reality:
* [William Spaniel](https://www.youtube.com/@Gametheory101): A professor of political science at the University of Pittsburgh who has made a ton of YouTube videos, pre-2022 mainly teaching game theory in technical terms and 2022-present mainly applying game theoretic concepts to understanding current international geopolitics.
* Timothy Snyder ([substack](https://snyder.substack.com/)) ([filmed course on Ukrainian history](https://online.yale.edu/courses/making-modern-ukraine)): A professor of History at Yale whose course I couldn't recommend enough. His substack focuses mostly on US politics, but in the context of history and what is happening in Europe now.
* [Uncommon Knowledge](https://www.hoover.org/publications/uncommon-knowledge): A podcast hosted by Hoover fellow and former Reagan speechwriter Peter Robinson. Features intelligent, balanced conversations with important and interesting guests.
