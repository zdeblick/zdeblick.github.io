I've spent a lot of time in this blog so far talking about very old democratic traditions, and in my last post I specifically called attention to the idea that democratic traditions evolved in a very different environment than today. In particular, the populations that participated in a single democratic system were very small compared to today's democratic states: the Athenian assembly (probably the largest of any like it until centuries later) typically consisted of tens of thousands of citizens residing in one city (Athens), while 642 *million* voters participated in India's last general election, spread out across an area almost 25 times larger than all of Greece. Try to imagine, for a second, an Athenian polis packed with 642 million citizens, all trying to participate in a single debate and enact a coherent policy. I actually struggle to imagine this, but I don't think it takes a lot of imagination to believe that this would not be an effective form of government. This is why, as states started to get bigger, they adapted existing democratic traditions like the assembly to serve larger and more spread out populations. In general, all have settled on some system of *representation*.

My computational neuroscientist alter-ego would tell you that representation is something the brain does all the time, when it uses some pattern of neural connectivity or activity to represent something in the real world. Why does the brain do this? Because it has to perform computations on visual, sound, and other input from the real world in order to choose appropriate (enhancing survival chances) behavior. So it transforms that input into some internal representation that is useful for performing computations (and giving computational neuroscientists something to study). 

Representative systems of government do essentially the same thing, for essentially the same reason. The basic input to a democracy is the will of the people, and the desired output is policy that maximizes some good property of those same people's lives (life, liberty, pursuit of happiness, stability, etc.). But for a large, diverse population, it is very difficult to incorporate the will of every person without somehow reducing the problem. So representative systems select a group of people small enough to actually debate and enact policy where each representative is selected to *represent* the will of some large group of voters. This essentially reduces the question of optimal democratic government to two mostly separate problems:

1. How to choose an appropriate set of representatives from a population?
2. What process should those representatives use to debate and enact laws?

The various democracies around the world have various answers to both of these questions, but all use this same basic idea. For the rest of today's post, we'll take a deeper dive into the first question, and in particular ask how good the USA's system is.

The USA has representatives at the federal, state, and local level. Except for the President, elected officials are selected state-by-state, and the states play a huge role for selecting the President too. (I've switched now to using "elected official" instead of "representative" because "Representative" refers to a specific type of elected official in America, but all elected officials represent some group of people.) Each state elects two Senators, each elected once every six years by voters across the entire state, and a number of Representatives proportional to the state's population (determining how exactly to apportion this number of Representatives to states is actually not a straightforward problem - I would highly reccommend checking out [this video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GVhFBujPlVo) to get into it). Up until 1842, each state had a lot of freedom in choosing how to elect their Representatives and two different approaches were used by different states: single-member districts and block voting. 

In block voting, if a state had *N* Representative seats apportioned to it, its ballot would allow voters to vote for *N* candidates, and the *N* candidates who received the most votes would be elected. In states that used this system, each political party put exactly *N* candidates on the ballot, and voters tended to vote for all *N* candidates of their party of preference. The result was that the majority party tended to take all *N* seats. 

In single-member districts, the system we have today, states draw a district map, dividing the state into $N$ regions. Then, in each region, a single-winner election takes place to elect the Representative from that district. Compared to block voting, this tends to produce a slate of Representatives from each state that includes members of multiple parties.

The presence of these two systems in different states gave an advantage to the party that tended to be the majority in states with block voting and the minority in states with single-member districts (the Federalists, over the Whigs). So when the disadvantaged Whigs gained control of Congress in 1842, they passed the [Apportionment Act](https://history.house.gov/Blog/2019/April/4-16-Apportionment-1/), standardizing the system of representation across states to use single-member districts. 

However, this change did not end each state's ability to meaningfully change their electoral system: which district map a state uses matters. Consider a state with 20 Representatives apportioned whose voters are 55% Orange party and 45% Pink party. A district map that maintains the same proportions in each of the 20 districts will elect 20 Orange representatives, while a map drawn such that each district is either 100% Orange or 100% Pink will elect 11 Orange representatives and 9 Pink ones, in proportion with the state's overall makeup. Even though both of these systems are single-member districts, one gives the same outcome as block voting. An even more extreme example: consider a district map where three districts are 100% Orange (accounting for 15% of the state's voters) and the other 17 districts are 45/85 $\approx$ 53% Pink party. With single-member districts, the state government (who chooses the district map) has almost total control over which Representatives get sent to Congress. Moreover, they can use the same process to elect their own state government, maintaining control of the maps and thereby the government, even with a small minority of the voters. The process of drawing district maps with the intent of achieving a result favorable to the map-drawers' political party is known as ["gerrymandering,"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrymandering) after a map drawn under Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry, featuring a district that looked like a salamander.

A state legislature choosing which lawmakers to send to Congress is not really all that undemocratic - it's actually how the US Constitution was written: until the 17th ammendment was ratified in 1913, this is how states chose Senators. However, a state's elected officials clinging to power through district maps even if they lose popular support does present a problem, because that is effectively an autocracy, where people in power choose who is in power, instead of all people. Another problem is if we reach a similar situation the Whigs and Federalists faced before 1842, where, at the national level, one party is given an unfair advantage by the different systems used in different states.

Soon, we're going to look at whether the US is currently facing such a problem, but first I want to dig into what one might even mean here by "unfair" advantages. After all, the US constitution is written to give a lot of power to the states: each state gets two Senators, no matter how populous. Just because one party has a majority of the popular vote, should they get complete control of government? I believe the US Constitution was written as it was for two main reasons:

1. To provide checks and balances between the branches of federal government, and between state and federal government, to make it harder for any effort to disenfranchise voters to succeed.
2. To empower political minorities.

The two points go hand in hand: because Senators, Representatives, Presidents, and officials of each state's government are elected in different ways, they tend to represent different groups of people. In general, all branches of federal and states' government are not totally controlled by the same party, making it harder for that party to get away with disenfranchising the voters. But it also means that, in general, the majority party must compromise with the minority in order to actually get stuff done, producing outcomes closer to what the median voter would want (see [my post on the median voter](https://zdeblick.github.io/2025/02/19/Cycles-in-Party-Platforms.html)). It's interesting to me that you hear plenty of calls to abolish the electoral college as a means of electing a President, and plenty to abolish gerrymandering, but not so many to abolish the Senate. Suffice it to say that, while there are almost certainly fairer ways to achieve a stable democracy that protects minorities, I think we should appreciate how the system we have does achieve this.

Given this, it's not really clear what "fairness" should mean. Chief Justice Roberts says as much in his 2019 opinion for a landmark gerrymandering case, *Rucho v. Common Cause*: "The initial difficulty in settling on a ‘clear manageable and politically neutral’ test for fairness is that it is not even clear what fairness looks like in this context. There is a large measure of ‘unfairness’ in any winner-take-all system." The fact that Roberts calls out winner-takes-all systems as being unfair makes me think that his idea of fairness of a representative system is similar to its *accuracy*, in the sense that each citizen gets a representative who accurately represents their views. This is not true in a winner-take-all system because all the people who voted for losers (often as many as 49% of voters) get a representative whom they did not vote for. So should the US House of Representatives be more accurate? What alternative system would help it be more accurate?

My opinion is that, yes, the US House of Representatives (and state legislatures too) should use a system that makes it more accurate, given that it is part of a bigger system with built-in checks and balances and protections for minorities. I believe this even though, as we'll see, the system is not very biased towards one party or the other. Because even if the system does not greatly advantage one party, it does disadvantage a blue voter in a red state or district or a red voter in a blue state or district, and it gives us many Representatives on both sides who do not need to be responsive to voters at all because of the district drawn for them.

The "gold standard" system for achieving accurate representation is Single Transferrable Vote, aka Proportional Ranked Choice Voting. If you are interested in fair representation and optimal government, I would highly reccommend checking it out (FairVote has a [great page with a nice explanatory video](https://fairvote.org/our-reforms/proportional-ranked-choice-voting-information/)). The bottom line is that multiple candidates are elected in a given election, like with block voting, but voters rank their preferences and an algorithm is used that iteratively selects and eliminates candidates, transferring votes to next-ranked candidates until a slate of candidates is elected that accurately reflect the population who elected them. 

There are many possible complaints about STV: "it's more complicated", "voters have to research more candidates to figure out how to vote", "it opens the door for third parties to come in and mess up the system that has been working so well with just two" (sarcasm from me here). But all of these are just growing pains, which will fade once voters and candidates are used to the new system. There's really only one complaint I've heard that is worth addressing here: districts make sure that non-voters are represented too by requiring districts to have comparable populations (not voting populations), while proportional representation only represents voters. This can be a real problem, given that there are certain minority groups (definied by ethnicity, language, disability, etc) that tend to have lower voter turnout. However, single-member districts only protect such minorities if they have their own districts, whereas a statewise system would allow a candidate representing a small, geographically diverse minority to have a chance, if there are enough seats. Still, when we look at data and think about accuracy of representation systems, we should account for the fact that not everyone votes, and that non-voters and voters tend to be different.

So now the main question, which we'll answer with a data-dive: how fair or unfair is the US House of Representatives? We can also see which states are doing an A+ job at representing their voters, and which are failing. Basically, we want to know, given how many people voted for each party in each state, how many Representatives should each party have gotten under a perfectly accurate system?

One complication we want to address is the fact that candidates are individuals, and a vote for them isn't really a vote for their party. However, party registration is inclomplete since many people vote in general elections who are not registered with a party. So instead I'm going to use votes cast in a presidential election, broken down by which congressional district the votes came from. Somewhat arbitrarily, I've gone with the Biden v Trump 2020 election, and used the most recent population estimates for the congressional districts. I want to thank [Daily Kos](https://www.dailykos.com/stories/2021/3/23/1220127/-Daily-Kos-Elections-statewide-election-results-by-congressional-and-legislative-districts#2022) for breaking down the election results by district and the [Census Bureau](https://data.census.gov/chart/ACSST1Y2021.S2901?q=S2901:+CITIZEN,+VOTING-AGE+POPULATION+BY+SELECTED+CHARACTERISTICS&g=010XX00US$5000000&type=bar&measure=LINE1&consts=dimension~BOXHEAD1) for the population estimates.

We can see that the fraction of actual votes for Biden in each state is not that different from what it would have been if the whole population had voted (using our extrapolated estimate), suggesting that the variability across districts in voter turnout does not tend to advantage either party (this is just a suggestion - it might be that turnout is lower among would-be blue or red voters, but it is hard to know that for sure since there is no voting record for non-voters).

However, the fraction of districts where a majority voted for Biden is very different in some states. Note that I'm still using presidential votes and not the actual result for the congressional race in each district for consistency, but in practice the results are not very different: there were 7 districts won by Trump and a Democrat Representative, and 9 by Biden and a Republican Representative. The trend we see for states where the fraction of blue votes and fraction of blue seats are very different is that states inflate their majorities. This is to be expected, assuming that the same party winning a majority of votes controls the maps, and it is even expected for randomly drawn maps, since winner-take-all elections tend to over-represent majorities (see [this study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.01735) for example - which also suggests that a random map favors Republicans because of the urban-rural distribution). However, it is also clear to see that some states do better than others.

To quantify this, I calculated the number of Gerrymandered seats in each state as the number of seats that would have to flip to achieve maximally accurate representation, given the number of seats apportioned to that state (Columns T and U, where U uses the extrapolated estimates; Negative means  advantaging Biden, positive means advantaging Trump). However, bigger states will naturally tend to have more Gerrymandered seats, so I also calculated a "Gerryscore" of how much the accuracy could be improved by flipping seats (Column Y; a higher number means worse representation).
