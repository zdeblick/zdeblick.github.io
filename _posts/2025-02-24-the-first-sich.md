The first choice that we face in designing our constitution for the planet Sich is this: should we have everybody vote? In other words, should our constitution create a democracy of some sort where every person has some ability to influence government, or should it create an autocracy of some sort where governance is left entirely to professionals, and the common people can just worry about whatever it is they do in their daily lives on Sich without having to think about government?

Now, there are many things worth considering in this decision, such as how each system can or cannot guarantee personal freedoms, what sort of economic conditions each creates, and what the implications are for relationships with other countries (okay, not on Sich, but if we were talking about a country on Earth). But today, I'm going to focus on one important consideration for whether a democracy can work and relate it to what is currently happening in Ukraine, as we reach three years since russia's full-scale invasion. That consideration is the title of this post: why does anybody vote at all?

Obviously, some people must vote for a democracy to function, but if you think closely, it is not obvious why any one person should vote. It's worth noting that in some countries, like Australia, voting is mandatory, with sufficient incentives that almost everyone votes. Even in these places, however, people must still take some time to figure out whom they should vote for (instead of just voting randomly) in order for democracy to succeed. A reason that is often given encouraging people to vote is that the thing you are voting for impacts you: if you elect candidate A, their decisions will impact you differently than those that candidate B would make. However, except in some very narrow circumstances, this logic doesn't hold up because *you* don't elect a candidate - the voting population does. And for your vote to make the difference, the election would need to be within a *one-vote margin*. It is hard to say exactly how likely you should think this is, because it depends on your beliefs about the rest of the electorate, but the point is it isn't very likely. According to [this study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1024244329828) on US elections between 1898 and 1992, "One of every 89,000 votes cast in U.S. Congressional elections, and one of 15,000 in state legislator elections, "mattered" in the sense that they were cast for a candidate that tied or won by one vote." If we just go with these empirical probabilities and say that casting a vote will take up $5's worth of our time (maybe it takes 15 minutes at $20/hr, a low estimate), then we should conclude that it is worth the time to vote only if you expect that your preferred congressional or state legislature candidate would make your life $445,000 or $75,000 better than the alternative if elected. Is this the case for most voters?

While these numbers are probably small enough to influence most voters' decision to vote, I don't think it's the main reason most people vote. Imagine that the state legislature race in your district that happened most recently had been a tie, and your ballot had been determined to be accidentally destroyed, so the court summoned you to testify on who you want to win. The candidate you didn't vote for approaches you and makes a guaranteeable offer of $75,000 if you say you voted for them. Most of us would have an ethical objection to accepting this offer, but try to set that aside and think if it would be a good financial decision. For most voters, I think it would be a great financial decision to accept that offer.

So why else might people vote? I think there are two overlapping reasons: personal values and social pressure. Personal values means that people vote because they derive some personal value in the act of voting, whether from feeling that they are part of something larger than themselves, honoring all those who protected their right to vote, or trying to meet somebody cute in line at the polling location. Social pressure means that people vote because others can tell whether they do, and impose consequences for not voting. This is the logic behind "I voted" stickers and a fortuitous byproduct of using [election ink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_ink). I say that the two reasons are overlapping because every individual's values are shaped by society. Feeling a part of something larger only makes sense if there are other people being part of it too. Even finding that cute person in line depends on them deciding to show up too.

But this presents a problem: *how does voting become important to individuals, or to society?* I think there are a number of plausible answers, including regional turnout as a signal to authorities about whose votes are worth chasing and early, low-turnout elections making voting more attractive by the probabilistic logic we just went through. The point I want to focus on, however, is that once a country has a democratic tradition for a longer time, it is less likely to fail. Some of this is probably thanks to slow improvements in other institutions, but probably a lot has to do with individuals getting accustomed to voting and society developing norms and institutions that get more people to vote (and not just vote, but try to make an informed vote).

This brings me to Ukraine, and to russia. There are many differences between Ukraine and russia that may explain why Ukraine developed as a democracy, while russia sank back into autocracy under Putin. Before I talk about any of them, I want to present one theory about why Putin chose to invade Ukraine. In this theory, Putin decided to invade because it was good for him (and his oligarchs and everyone else who benefits from russia being an autocracy) and not necessarily for the Russian people. Why would Putin take the personal risk that the Russian people would get upset about the war and put political pressure on him? Because he calculated that it would be a greater risk for him to not invade. There are several possible reasons for this, including using Ukraine to disrupt potential military coups in russia, by moving the plotters far away and letting Ukraine kill them (the Prigozhin saga is evidence for this idea). The risk that Ukraine is uniquely involved in, however, is a democratic revolution in russia. I say Ukraine is involved not because it would participate in this revolution, but because it would inspire it. For all the differences between russia and Ukraine, there is enough similarity between Russians and Ukrainians, and Ukraine is transparent enough from a typical Russian's perspective, that enough typical Russians in 2022 might start saying, "Hey, Ukraine is turning into a great country! The median income is about the same as here, they have all these democratic freedoms that we don't, and soon they'll have all these benefits from integrating with Europe.". Putin invaded Ukraine in the hopes of destroying it so that it could no longer be an example for these Russians, and so that russia would seem strong by comparison. Of course, both of these aims backfired because of how well Ukraine has been able to resist - while they have had to enact martial law and suspend some freedoms (including elections) in order to protect the public, there is no doubt that they are a strong democracy, and their military has embarrassed one that was recently thought to be the second most powerful in the world. In order to cope, Putin first tried mobilizing more Russians, and then after hundreds of thousands fled russia to escape the draft, he is trying to use North Koreans on the battlefield. Putin also felt threatened enough by the idea of democracy in russia that he decided to murder Alexei Navalny, its most prominent advocate.

I want to return to the question of democratic tradition and how it might have gotten started in Ukraine, whereas in russia it was weak enough to be derailed. I am not a historian, and the histories of Ukraine and russia are both too complicated to do this question justice. But I want to share one piece of the answer that is definitely important to Ukrainians: the Cossacks. The Cossacks emerged as an important geopolitical entity around the 15th century, but may have existed in some form as early as the 8th, before the establishment of Kyivan Rus around 880. Ukraine, russia, and Belarus all trace their national identity back to Kyivan Rus, although, as the name suggests, the heart of Rus was in modern-day Ukraine. That russia and Belarus contain the root "rus" in their names has to do with a clever heist of the name, where modern-day russia was first Greater Rus (as in Outer Rus) and the area around Kyiv was Central Rus, and then those in power in Moscow around 1654 rebranded to being "Great Rus" (with the area around Kyiv being "Little Rus" and modern-day Belarus being "White Rus" (bela- comes from the root for white). However, there is a shared connection to Rus up until the Mongol invasion destroyed Rus as a political power in 1240 - from that point on, the peoples of modern-day Ukraine and russia had very different histories.

The connection to Kyivan Rus is important to this discussion because democratic practices were common there, in particular the *viche*. The viche was a public assembly open to all free men (or in some cases possibly just the nobles) employing public debate and majority vote on important matters and for the election of various authorities. There is reference to the practice before 1000, and it may be much older, as early references refer to it as "from of old." After the fallout of the Mongol invasion, much of modern-day Ukraine became part of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, while the area around Moscow remained under weakening Mongol control, eventually giving way to competition among the principalities, with Moscow coming out on top.

The Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth maintained the practice of viches and ultimately established the Sejm, the national legislature, which eventually started to employ publicly elected representatives in 1468, giving rise to a bottom-up system of power, where the people exert a measure of control over the monarchy. Through this period, viches continued to happen in some principalities under Mongol control, but once Ivan III consolidated power in Moscow, he began abolishing the practice around 1500. Perhaps this is a moment where Moscow could have stayed on a democratic path, instead of adopting the Mongol's methods of supporting an emperor by using new plunder and land from continual conquest to sustain a hierarchical system of top-down control.

This is around the time when the Cossacks start to become more important as an independent political force. The origin of Cossacks is complicated, but suffice it to say that they were ethnically diverse, Orthodox Christian, shared the status of "free men" (as opposed to nobles, serfs, etc.), and organized military defense of their land around fortresses called *Sich*es. Eventually, the word "Sich" came to refer more symbolically to Cossack society and government. The word "Cossack" meant "free man" in Turkic, and this concept is indeed central to the Cossack way of life. Firstly, Cossacks sought to establish independence from imperial control. In the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, Cossacks demanded more autonomy from the central government and eventually gained independent status as the *Cossack Hetmanate* in 1649 with the Treaty of Zboriv after they rebelled against Poland in the Khmelnytsky Uprising. The Hetmanate was the first thing since the fall of Rus that could rightly be called a Ukrainian state, controlling [much of modern-day Ukraine's territory](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/picturedisplay.asp?linkpath=pic%5CC%5CO%5CCossack_Hetman_State_after_1649.jpg&page=pages%5CH%5CE%5CHetmanstate.htm&id=4132&pid=3748&tyt=Hetman%20state&key=Hetman+state%2C+Hetmanshchyna). Due to geopolitical necessity, the Hetmanate had to compromise its independence by formally accepting subordinate status under, at different times, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Ottoman Empire, and the russian empire. However, the desire to be free from imperial control over their own affairs remained a defining characteristic, one that is inherited by today's Ukraine. Secondly, the people who became Cossacks came from many different backgrounds, escaping various forms of control by various powers, but all accepted the common status of Cossack (free man). This created the conditions for a democracy among equals, and indeed the Hetmanate generally governed and elected its leader, the hetman, through a [Council of Cossacks](https://www.encyclopediaofukraine.com/display.asp?linkpath=pages%5CG%5CE%5CGeneralMilitaryCouncil.htm). In 1710, when Ben Franklin, the oldest author of the US Constitution, was four years old, Hetman Pylyp Orlyk and other Cossacks wrote a constitution asserting independence from Poland and russia, limiting the power of the hetman, protecting the rights of towns, and limiting taxation of the poor. However, the Hetmanate was not able to remain independent of russian influence, which increasingly limited Ukrainian self-governance and ultimately abolished the Hetmanate altogether in 1764-5.

There is so much more to the story connecting this democratic legacy through the Cossacks to today's Ukraine. One could spend a career chronicling:

* the literary, artistic, and historical work preserving and developing Ukrainian culture through periods of statelessness,

* the political and military resistance to the russian emipre/soviet union and the brutal massacres and forced starvation of millions of Ukrainians by the same,

* the development of the Ukrainian language alongside all the others that branched from Old Church Slavonic and its suppression by the russian empire,

* and the struggle of Ukrainians to protect their reclaimed democracy from russian influence in the Orange Revolution, Euromaidan, and the russian occupation of Crimea and the Donbas.

The important thing for us to note today, though, is simply this: for Ukrainians, the democratic legacy prevails. Despite the efforts of russia through the centuries, Ukrainian language, Ukrainian culture, the Ukrainian state, and the Ukrainian democratic tradition are alive. Not only have Ukrainians shown up to vote, they have shown up to protest when they felt that their government was representing them, even putting their lives in danger to do so. This is the reason russia has failed, and will continue to fail, to control Ukraine. Sadly, I believe it is also why Putin feels he must try to do so: because the russian empire, soviet union, and Putin's russia have all maintained power through suppression of the people's voice.

As an American, I believe we also benefit from this democratic tradition. Because we have been a democracy for so long, I think it will be harder for those who would do so to convert our state into an authoritarian regime like russia. In 2010, the Ukrainian people fairly elected the pro-russian candidate Victor Yanukovych as president, despite him having been shown guilty of trying to steal the 2004 election from Victor Yushchencko, who was also poisoned during the campaign. And yet in 2014, enough Ukrainians showed up to protest his broken promises and violent repression, risking their lives, to force his removal and new elections. Today, I am as disappointed as I have ever been in our president, Donald Trump. In his first weeks, he has sought to concentrate power under himself by doing a host of things through executive order that should be done by an Act of Congress, if not an amendment to our Constitution. But today, under his authority, the United States voted against the resolution put forward by Ukraine and our European allies condemning russia's invasion and calling for russia to cease its war of aggression. While in my understanding, this is a legal act on Trump's part, it is a horrifying one - rejecting the clear allocation of blame in favor of his own draft resolution which treats Ukraine and russia as equally culpable. However, I believe that America's democratic tradition will ultimately prevail - that we will defend our Constitution while strengthening our critical institutions that protect its power. This is something we can all participate in by protesting (with the gratitude that our government does not persecute us for doing so), writing to our elected representatives, and voting every time we have the chance. By doing these things, we affirm that we, as individuals, value our democratic freedoms. And by encouraging our friends and family to do these things, we use social pressure to make sure that our society values these freedoms collectively. I believe in America, and I believe that these things will happen.

I just hope that they happen in time to stop russia and its friends from destroying other democracies that do not have as strong a military as ours (like Ukraine) or as strong a democratic tradition.
